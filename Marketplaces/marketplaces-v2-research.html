The Fear Underneath the Fear
“I’ll spend money and time getting set up and nothing will happen.” — Setup effort with no guaranteed return. This is the real objection. Everything else is rationalisation.
What They Don’t Know — Opportunity Reframes
  • 60% of Australians purchased from Amazon in the past 12 months. These sellers are invisible to that traffic.

  • Unauthorised third-party resellers may already be selling their products on Amazon — without their control over pricing, listing quality, or brand reputation.
  • Amazon accounted for nearly 10% of all online shopping in Australia in 2024. Projected to double by 2026.
  • Amazon is now a top-3 product search engine in Australia — people use it to find products, not just to buy things they’ve already decided on.

Audience 2 — Amazon Sellers NOT Running PPC
Why they’re not running ads — in their own words
“I tried PPC once and spent $500 with nothing to show for it”
“I’m already getting organic sales — why would I pay for ads?”

The Reality Behind the Hesitation
Australia’s lower search volume means keyword signals take 60–90 days to stabilise. Sellers who try PPC without strategy burn budget early and conclude “PPC doesn’t work here.” It’s not that PPC doesn’t work — they ran it wrong once and stopped. Amazon’s SQP (Search Query Performance) data gets suppressed at lower AU volumes, making it harder to optimise without knowing what you’re doing.
What They Don’t Know — Opportunity Reframes
  • Without PPC, competitors ARE running Sponsored ads against their listings and stealing Buy Box visibility.
  • “In Australia, PPC is about efficiency not aggression” — lower competition = lower CPCs when run correctly.
  • PPC data reveals what customers are actually searching — organic keyword optimisation without it is guesswork.
  • Listing suppression happens silently; PPC is often the first signal something’s wrong with a listing.
  • A well-managed AU campaign should be profitable within 60–90 days.

Assumptions Identified

Strip everything the offer currently takes for granted.

ASSUMPTION 01
The target audience is correctly identified. We assume these owners are “already sold” on Amazon and just need execution help — that the decision is made.
ASSUMPTION 02
The primary barrier is complexity/time. We assume the reason they haven’t started is operational friction, not financial concern, doubt about ROI, or risk aversion.
ASSUMPTION 03
30 days is the meaningful milestone. We assume getting listed within 30 days is the outcome the prospect values. That “live on Amazon” equals “sales increase.”
ASSUMPTION 04
The guarantee removes the main objection. We assume “work for free” meaningfully de-risks the decision and is the mechanism that converts hesitation to action.
ASSUMPTION 05
$1,500 setup is priced correctly relative to the DIY alternative. We assume the $6,700 DIY cost creates strong perceived value for a $1,500 managed setup.
ASSUMPTION 06
The retainer is justified by ongoing management complexity. We assume the client will see enough value in month 2, 3, 4 to continue paying $1,000/month after setup.
ASSUMPTION 07
Sales will actually increase. We assume that simply listing products on Amazon will generate meaningful incremental revenue — enough to satisfy both the guarantee and the ongoing retainer.
ASSUMPTION 08
“Other marketplaces” adds value to the offer. We assume multi-marketplace positioning strengthens the offer rather than diffusing it.
ASSUMPTION 09
The prospect sees removing the free audit as neutral or positive. We assume the audit removal doesn’t increase friction in the buying process.
ASSUMPTION 10
Amazon is the right primary marketplace to lead with. We assume Amazon AU is the highest-leverage channel for this audience’s product categories.
ASSUMPTION 11
These owners are not already working with another agency on this. We assume they are unserved or self-reliant, not mid-process with a competitor.
ASSUMPTION 12
The economic climate creates urgency. We assume “revenue diversification” is an active emotional driver, not background noise.
Base Truths

What remains after stripping assumptions — verifiable, factual, logical.

T1
40% of AU online sales occur on marketplaces. This is a structural market fact, not a trend.
T2
Amazon AU has 60% buyer penetration and 66% Prime membership. The demand-side infrastructure exists and is mature.
T3
The minimum cost to self-manage an Amazon launch is approximately $6,700 (tools + GTINs + Vine + launch spend). This is a verifiable cost floor.
T4
Getting products listed on Amazon is not the same as getting products ranked and selling on Amazon. These are distinct problems requiring distinct activities.
T5
Amazon’s marketplace is competitive. Most product categories have existing sellers. A new listing does not automatically generate sales — discoverability requires PPC investment, review velocity, and listing optimisation that takes time to compound.
T6
A 30-day listing guarantee is deliverable. A 30-day sales increase guarantee is not — sales on Amazon typically lag listing by weeks to months depending on category, review count, and PPC ramp.
T7
The ongoing operational work post-setup (inventory sync, PPC management, listing optimisation, review monitoring) is real and recurring. The retainer is defensible if scoped correctly.
T8
Shopify/WooCommerce owners have existing product catalogues, images, and descriptions. The data already exists — the friction is technical integration and Amazon-specific requirements (compliance, GTIN, content formatting), not content creation from scratch.
T9
Agency retention in services is driven by demonstrable results, not by the ongoing existence of tasks. A client who sees no sales lift will not renew at $1,000/month regardless of management activity.
T10
The audience pool of “already sold, hasn’t started” is smaller than the pool of “considering but not committed.” Targeting only the former caps volume.
Rebuild from Base Truths

The actual product being sold is not “Amazon setup.” It is “incremental revenue from an untapped channel.” The client doesn’t care about being listed — they care about sales that wouldn’t have existed otherwise.


From T4 and T5: getting listed in 30 days is a setup milestone, not a revenue milestone. The 30-day guarantee promises the wrong thing. A client who is live on Amazon with zero sales after 30 days has technically received the service but practically received nothing. The guarantee, as worded, could be fully honoured while still failing the client’s actual goal.


From T6: the phrase “increase your sales” in the guarantee creates legal and commercial exposure. Sales on Amazon in month 1 will almost always be lower than month 3 or month 6, because the algorithm rewards history. The guarantee implies sales increase within 30 days, which contradicts how the channel actually works.


From T3 and T8: the $6,700 DIY cost is a genuine and powerful anchor — but only if the prospect understands the components of that cost. Most won’t. The comparison needs unpacking to land. $1,500 flat looks cheap against $6,700 DIY only when the breakdown is visible.


From T9: the retainer’s survival depends on demonstrable monthly progress. If the offer doesn’t include a reporting or results layer, clients will churn at month 2–3 when the setup excitement fades and results are uneven.


From T7 and T8: the real moat for Phase 3 is not “we can list products” (that’s procedural). The moat is “we know how to optimise, rank, and sustain sales on Amazon AU specifically” — that is scarcer and harder to DIY than the setup itself.


Rebuilt offer logic: The strongest version of this offer leads with the revenue outcome, explains the 30-day timeline as the launch phase, frames the retainer as the growth phase, and makes the guarantee specific enough to be credible and broad enough to be meaningful. “Listed in 30 days” is a milestone, not a promise. “Generating incremental revenue within 90 days” is the promise that aligns with how the channel actually works.

Divergence from Original Thinking
Divergence 1 — The Guarantee Promises the Wrong Thing & Creates Risk

Original: “increase your sales... within 30 days or we work for free.” Problem: Amazon does not produce meaningful sales results in 30 days for a cold listing. PPC needs at least 2–4 weeks of data before it optimises. Vine reviews take 30+ days. Organic rank takes months. Honouring this guarantee literally is easy (you got them listed); honouring it in spirit is nearly impossible in 30 days for most SKUs.

The divergence matters because: Either (a) the guarantee will be triggered frequently and erode margin/trust, or (b) clients will feel they got listed but not what they actually wanted, increasing churn and negative word-of-mouth.

Fix: Reframe as “listed and live within 30 days, or your setup fee is refunded.” Keep the guarantee tied to what you can control. The revenue story lives in the retainer pitch, not the guarantee.

Divergence 2 — “Increase Your Sales” Is Unverifiable Without a Baseline

Original: Implies sales go up within 30 days. Problem: If the client’s Shopify store has a good month due to a promotion or a seasonal spike, does that count? If their Amazon channel does $200 in month 1, has sales “increased”? The guarantee has no defined baseline, no defined channel scope, and no defined time window for measurement.

The divergence matters because: Ambiguous guarantees create disputes and erode trust faster than no guarantee at all.

Divergence 3 — The Audience Framing Narrows the Addressable Market Unnecessarily

Original: “NOT fence-sitters needing education.” Problem: The line between “already sold but hasn’t started” and “nearly convinced but needs the last push” is thin and largely invisible from the outside. Eliminating education from the messaging excludes a significant portion of the addressable market who are 80% there and just need the value crystallised.

The divergence matters because: The “already sold” cohort is a small, hard-to-identify segment. Broadening slightly to include “almost sold” owners would meaningfully increase the prospect pool without changing the product.

Divergence 4 — The Cost-Comparison Anchor Is Underleveraged

Original: $6,700 DIY cost is mentioned as context, not as a central messaging element. Problem: This is the single most persuasive number in the brief. $1,500 versus $6,700 for the same outcome (being live on Amazon) is a 4.5x cost advantage before the first dollar of retainer. The ad scripts don’t appear to foreground this. It should be a primary hook in at least one dedicated execution.

Divergence 5 — “Other Marketplaces” is Vague and Dilutes the Amazon Narrative. Amazon AU is the only marketplace with meaningful data in this brief (60% buyer penetration, Prime, projected growth). Adding “other marketplaces” without naming them or quantifying their impact makes the offer feel padded rather than comprehensive. Either name them specifically with supporting data (eBay AU: X%, Catch: X%), or lead with Amazon and position others as “Phase 2” or an upsell. Vague scope weakens conviction.

Recommended Actions
1
Rewrite the guarantee
“We’ll have your products live on Amazon within 30 days, or your setup fee is fully refunded.” This is 100% within your control, easy to honour, and still removes the execution risk the prospect fears. The revenue story is told separately — in the retainer pitch and in client case studies, not in the guarantee clause.
2
Build a visible retainer roadmap
Build a visible “Month 1 / Month 2–3 / Month 6+” roadmap into the offer collateral. Clients need to know what they are paying $1,000/month for. Without this, the retainer feels like a fee for listing maintenance. With it, it reads as a growth programme.
3
Lead the $6,700 DIY cost comparison more aggressively
Lead the $6,700 DIY cost comparison more aggressively in ad creative and the landing page. It’s the clearest articulation of value in the entire brief and currently treated as background context. Consider a dedicated script or ad unit built entirely around this number.
4
Relax the “no fence-sitters” constraint in ad targeting
The landing page can remain conversion-focused for the decided buyer. The ads can cast slightly wider — the messaging will self-select; anyone who clicks “I want this done for me” was close enough.
5
Commit to naming the secondary marketplaces or simplify to Amazon-first
Either commit to naming the secondary marketplaces with real data, or simplify to “Amazon-first” with a stated expansion path. Do not let “other marketplaces” float undefined in final copy — it creates unanswered questions at the decision point.
6
Add a 6th script built around the $6,700 cost comparison
The existing scripts are directionally sound. None of them need to be discarded. The most important addition is a 6th script built around the $6,700 cost comparison — this is the only angle not covered and potentially the strongest rational hook in the set.

Weird But Potentially Brilliant
UNUSUAL
W1 — “Amazon doesn’t care about your brand story”

Why uncomfortable: It cuts against every brand-building narrative DTC founders hold sacred. The brand story is their identity. Telling them it doesn’t matter on Amazon feels like an attack.

Why it might be brilliant: It reframes Amazon not as a lesser channel but as a deliberately different one — pure product-market fit, no branding overhead. For founders who’ve spent years crafting their DTC brand, this is liberating, not threatening. Amazon is where the product sells itself.

Use case: Contrarian blog post angle. Could perform well in organic social for engagement/debate.

UNUSUAL
W2 — “Your Shopify store is the backup plan”

Why uncomfortable: It inverts the client’s entire mental model. They built their Shopify store. It’s their main thing. Calling it the backup plan feels wrong.

Why it might be brilliant: It’s accurate. Most buyers price-check and discover on Amazon, then sometimes go to DTC for reorders. Framing Shopify as the retention engine and Amazon as the acquisition engine is a genuine strategic insight — and it would stop a Shopify owner mid-scroll.

Use case: Landing page hero for a bold agency brand.

UNUSUAL
W3 — The Exit Valuation Angle

Why uncomfortable: Talking about selling your business when the offer is just “list your products” feels like a big leap.

Why it might be brilliant: Business owners who are 3–5 years in think about exit, even if they don’t say it. Multi-channel revenue commands better multiples. If someone is building toward a sale, this reframes the $1,000/month retainer as an investment in a higher exit price. One sentence. Massive leverage.

Use case: Best placed as a secondary callout or blog angle, not the hero.

Pass 1 — Surface
PASS 1 — SURFACE

The offer is competitively priced and structurally sound. $1,500 setup + $1,000/month retainer is accessible enough to convert Shopify-only brands that have been stalling on Amazon for months or years. The revised guarantee (“products live within 30 days or full setup refund”) eliminates the most obvious objection — “what if it doesn’t work?” — without creating an undeliverable promise tied to sales performance.

The creative research has already done the hard work: five validated angles, clear audience segments, and council verdicts that map each angle to a channel. The $6,700 DIY anchor is genuinely strong — it reframes the $1,500 fee from a cost into a discount. The “Invisible Customer” angle addresses a real cognitive blind spot: DTC founders don’t mentally model Amazon as their customer’s search engine.

At surface level, the primary task is execution sequencing: land on one hero angle for the page, one angle per ad script, write the blog and social around the same core idea so the message reinforces across touchpoints rather than fragments.

The obvious recommendation: lead with Invisible Customer on the page, use $6,700 Math in ads, and publish blog + social to drive traffic into the funnel.

Pass 2 — Underneath
The surface recommendation is correct but incomplete. Here’s what’s actually shaping the outcomes beneath it.
INSIGHT 1
The real objection isn’t cost or complexity — it’s identity.
Shopify founders who haven’t launched on Amazon haven’t done it because they’ve decided, implicitly or explicitly, that Amazon is “not them.” They associate Amazon with commoditisation, race-to-the-bottom pricing, and losing brand control. The $6,700 Math and Invisible Customer angles both bypass this entirely — they argue economics and visibility. Neither addresses the identity block. The Identity Gap angle (Angle E) is being parked in social, but it may be the unlock for the hardest segment: brands that are genuinely resistant, not just procrastinating.
INSIGHT 2
The guarantee creates a new sales conversation it doesn’t finish.
“Live within 30 days” solves the delivery trust problem but opens a new question: “What happens after live?” A brand that gets listed in 30 days and sees zero sales by day 60 will feel misled — even though the guarantee was technically honoured. The retainer has no visible architecture. Month 2 looks exactly like Month 1 from the outside, which means churn pressure starts immediately after the setup excitement fades.
INSIGHT 3
“Other marketplaces” is active harm, not just vagueness.
Leaving marketplace scope undefined on the landing page doesn’t just reduce conviction — it actively invites the wrong conversation. A prospect who reads “Amazon and other marketplaces” and asks “what other marketplaces?” during a sales call now has a negotiation lever: “Can you add eBay for the same price?” Every unspecified element becomes a negotiation. Name them (eBay AU, Catch) or remove them.
INSIGHT 4
The “almost sold” audience is the highest-conversion segment and we’re not addressing them.
Owners who nearly pulled the trigger on Amazon six months ago are not cold prospects. They’ve already done their own research, already want to be there, and are stuck on a specific friction point (time, complexity, or fear of getting it wrong). Their objection is different: not “should I be on Amazon” but “can I trust someone else to do this right.” The current creative is aimed at cold-to-warm. Nothing speaks directly to the almost-sold segment, which is the segment most likely to convert fastest.
INSIGHT 5
The retainer pricing structure doesn’t justify itself.
$1,000/month after $1,500 setup sounds like a reasonable number, but there’s no stated logic for why it’s $1,000. What does that month buy? Until the retainer has a visible deliverable structure — even at a high level — it’s a number the prospect has to rationalise alone. Unanswered “why does it cost that” questions become objections the sales call has to handle instead of the page.
Pass 3 — System + Root
PASS 3 — SYSTEM + ROOT

The deeper issue isn’t any single angle or offer element. It’s that this is a productised service being sold into a market that doesn’t have a shared mental model for what “managed marketplace presence” means.

Accountants, web developers, and SEO agencies all have established service categories with understood deliverables and pricing norms. “Amazon management” does not. Every prospect arrives at the page with a different, mostly wrong, mental model of what they’re buying. Some think they’re paying for a listing. Some think they’re buying a growth partner. Some think they’re outsourcing a channel the way they outsource bookkeeping. These aren’t just different awareness levels — they’re structurally different purchase decisions with different objectors, different approval processes, and different definitions of success.

This is why the offer keeps needing to work so hard: it’s not just selling the service, it’s simultaneously defining the category it occupies. Every creative angle (Math, Invisible Customer, Reviews, Identity, Guarantee) is doing double duty — it’s both a persuasion mechanism and a category definition. That’s too much to ask of a headline.

The root cause of retainer churn (when it happens) will be this same misalignment: the brand bought “get us on Amazon” but the agency is delivering “ongoing marketplace management.” These are different services. When Month 2 looks like ongoing optimisation to the agency and “are you done yet?” to the client, the relationship breaks.

STRUCTURAL FIX 1
The page needs a three-sentence category definition before the angles do their work. Not a mission statement — a category frame. Something that tells the prospect what kind of thing they are buying before they encounter the price. Without this, every clever angle is landing in a vacuum.
STRUCTURAL FIX 2
The guarantee is positioned as a risk-reversal when it’s actually a category signal. A 30-day delivery guarantee in a vague market says: “this is a defined, repeatable process.” That’s more powerful than risk-reversal, but it only lands if the offer communicates process clarity. Right now the guarantee is doing the work alone.
Synthesis
THREE-PASS SYNTHESIS

Pass 1 said: pick the best angle, sequence the content, execute cleanly.

Pass 3 reveals: the angles are downstream of a category definition problem. Without a category frame, the angles compete with each other in the prospect’s mind instead of building a unified conviction. The retainer will churn at Month 2 not because of weak creative but because of misaligned expectations baked in at the sale.

What changes: The landing page needs a category-defining statement before the hero angle fires. The retainer needs a Month 1 / Month 2–3 / Month 6+ roadmap — not as a nice-to-have, but as a structural defence against churn that starts at onboarding. The guarantee language needs to signal process confidence, not just refund policy. The “almost sold” segment needs its own entry point, probably a distinct ad set rather than a separate page.

The surface answer (lead with Invisible Customer, use Math in ads) is still correct. But the execution without the category frame will underperform.

Step Deliverable Why this order
1 Landing page copy (draft) Everything else points here — ads, blog, social all need a destination before publishing
2 Blog post Adds SEO value immediately; provides a credibility asset to link from social before ads run; no budget required
3 Meta video Script 1 ($6,700 Math) Highest-confidence cold creative; test spend before running three scripts simultaneously
4 Social Posts 1 + 3 (LinkedIn) Organic reach, zero cost, tests message resonance before paid amplification
5 Meta video Scripts 2 + 3 Roll in once Script 1 has initial performance data; don’t split budget three ways on day one
6 Social Posts 2 + 4 + 5 Post 5 (almost-sold) performs best once there is retargeting traffic from ads — sequence it last
7 Landing page live + ads active Final step; don’t run paid traffic to an untested page
Critical: Do not publish ads before the landing page is live and QA’d on mobile. The funnel breaks at the page, not the ad.
